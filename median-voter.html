The median voter theorem (electoral version)

Two candidates who care only about winning choose positions on a policy line, and every voter votes for the closer one. Where do the candidates end up? The guided walkthrough works through one small electorate step by step, from counting votes to the equilibrium, and then adds a tenth voter to see what an even number of voters changes. Explore lets you build your own electorate, move the candidates, and check where a candidate can win and whether a pair of positions is an equilibrium. Practice has thirteen cases in seven groups, including voters spread evenly and a candidate who cares about policy.

Throughout, voters have symmetric preferences, so each votes for the closer candidate, and a voter exactly halfway between the candidates flips a coin. A candidate gets 1 for winning and 0 for losing, and a tied vote is settled by a coin toss. When coin flips can decide the election, what a candidate cares about is its probability of winning, which is its expected payoff (1 × the probability of winning + 0 × the probability of losing).

Practice

Thirteen cases in seven groups, each with its own voters. Most cases ask five to seven questions in the same order: find the median, count the votes, decide whether any indifferent voters are pivotal and, where they are, work out a probability of winning, check some moves, find the challenger’s winning region, and decide whether a pair of positions is an equilibrium. The cases with voters spread evenly ask about vote shares, and the last case has a candidate who cares about policy. A case is marked with a check once you answer every question right the first time.

Select every move that helps, or “None of them.”

Accompanies the Week 2 notes on the median voter (majority rule and the median, just enough game theory, the Hotelling–Downs model, the classroom simulation, and what the electoral theorem assumes). The electorates in the walkthrough, the explore presets and the practice cases are new, and none of them appears in the notes or the problem bank. Policy-motivated candidates who are unsure where the median voter is (the Calvert–Wittman model) are the subject of a separate tool.
